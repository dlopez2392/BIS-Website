Every practice management vendor now has an AI feature, and most of the demos are genuinely impressive. The demo is not the thing to evaluate. What matters is what happens on an ordinary Tuesday, when a patient calls about a refill and mentions a symptom, and the software has to decide what to do with that sentence.

Here is what we ask on behalf of a clinic before anything gets signed.

## 1. What does it do when someone describes a symptom?

This is the question that sorts the field. A scheduler should book, confirm, remind, and answer practical things like hours and what to bring. The moment a caller starts describing how they feel, the correct behaviour is to stop and hand the call to a person — and to tell the caller that is happening, so they are not left explaining themselves to something that stopped listening.

A vendor who cannot show you that path, live, has not thought about it.

## 2. Whose computers is the conversation running on?

"We use AI" is not an answer. You are entitled to the name of the company whose model processes what your patients say, whether that content is used to train anything, and how long it is kept. Those are contractual questions with yes-or-no answers. Vendors who need a week to find out are telling you something about how carefully it was set up.

## 3. Does it speak Spanish, or does it translate?

In the Valley this is not a courtesy feature. A patient who switches to Spanish mid-sentence should be answered in Spanish, in the register a person actually speaks — not with an English script run through a translator, which is obvious within about two lines and tells the caller exactly how much thought went into serving them.

## 4. What happens when it is wrong?

It will be. The useful question is whether a mistake is visible. Does someone at the practice see what was said? Is there a record you can read after the fact? A system that books quietly and fails quietly is worse than a voicemail, because a voicemail at least leaves evidence.

## 5. Who is accountable for compliance?

Nobody can sell you HIPAA compliance in a box, and a vendor implying otherwise is the wrong vendor. Compliance is a property of your whole practice: who has access, how it is limited, what agreements exist with anyone touching protected information, and whether it is documented well enough for your compliance counsel to review rather than take on faith.

What a good vendor can do is design the workflow so that protected information stays in systems you control, and hand your counsel something to read. That is a different promise, and it is the honest one.

## The short version

Ask what it refuses to do. A scheduler that knows where its job ends is worth more than one that answers everything.
